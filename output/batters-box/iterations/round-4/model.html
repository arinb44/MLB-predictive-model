What goes in

Team form

Rolling windows

Win percentage, run differential and runs scored over trailing windows — computed strictly from games before the one being predicted.

Rest

Days and bullpen

Days of rest, plus bullpen fatigue measured as extra innings absorbed in the trailing three days.

Park

Trailing park factor

Trailing park scoring average against the league average, estimated from this dataset rather than imported.

Pitcher

Starter identity

Starting-pitcher identity via Retrosheet (2015–2023), plus a trailing runs-allowed-in-own-starts proxy.

Elo

Written from scratch

An Elo rating that updates online after every game, so it needs no training split of its own.

Not used

Platoon splits

Starting-pitcher splits against opposing lineup handedness are not implemented. Neither is anything from during or after a game.

Three models, one problem

Logistic regression is the baseline. Gradient-boosted trees were expected to win on tabular data like this. The PyTorch model learns entity embeddings for team and starting-pitcher identity — thousands of distinct pitchers against thirty teams, the highest-cardinality input in the project — concatenated with the same numeric features under an MLP head, with a hand-written training loop.

The ceiling is the point

A realistic ceiling for MLB game prediction is about 55–58% accuracy. Game-to-game variance in baseball is high, and a model claiming much better than that is almost certainly leaking information or overfitting. All four models land inside that band, which is a sanity check in itself: a leaky pipeline tends to show up as suspiciously good accuracy.

What matters here is calibration, not raw accuracy. A model that says 60% should be right about 60% of the time across every prediction it makes at that number.

No leakage. Splits are chronological, never random k-fold, and every feature is a trailing window computed from games before the one being predicted — enforced by tests/test_features.py, not just claimed here.
